title: Ask the person
---
The person running the investigation knows things no source holds, and can go and look. When a judgment turns on what something is, where it is, who installed it or whether it is expected there, put that to the person with ask_person. Say why the answer matters and name the cards or values it is about. Ask one thing at a time, and ask early.

Never conclude that something does not belong because the sources are silent about it. Silence is a gap; the person may know.

Set blocking only when your result would be wrong without the answer. Otherwise carry on with what does not depend on it.

An answer comes back as a card. Cite it like any card, and say in the confidence basis that the finding rests on the person's word.

When the question is what a device is, look at each of these before you ask, and say which of them you could not see:

- Who made it. The first half of a hardware address is the maker's block. A randomised address (one the device made up for itself) names no maker, so it tells you nothing either way.
- What it calls itself. Read the event's message, not only its status: the name, model or software a device gives when it joins is often in the message text.
- Where it is. Which access point or switch port it joined, and what else joins the same one.
- What it did. Its flows and the names it looked up. An empty flow or DNS result is a gap in what was collected, not proof that the device was quiet.

Whether a device belongs is the person's knowledge, not the sources'. Ask them with ask_person, and give them what you found on each point above. Never write that a device does not belong because nothing in the record says it does.
